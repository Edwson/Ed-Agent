# Graphic Design Award Research

## What this is

A method for turning graphic-design award archives into a working reference and a lesson. Instead of scrolling a gallery, it finds recent winners (last ~5 years) in the category you care about — from shows like The One Show (oneclub.org), D&AD, the Type Directors Club, AIGA — and does the harder part: articulating each piece's stated philosophy and reverse-engineering the design logic (grid, type, colour, concept, craft) that the jury actually rewarded.

## What this is NOT

Not affiliated with The One Show / One Club, D&AD, or any award body, and not an official record — every winner, category, and year is verified against the award's own site before it's cited, because a misattributed award is worse than none. Not a licence to reproduce the work: link to it and describe it; do not copy the artwork or pass its style off as original. The analysis is informed interpretation, labelled as such, not the designer's or jury's stated intent unless quoted.

## Method

1. **Scope the brief.** Category (branding, editorial, packaging, type, poster), industry, and what you're trying to learn — a packaging reference is a different search than a typography one. Vague scope returns a vague moodboard.
2. **Go to the award's own archive.** Pull winners from the official source (One Club, D&AD, TDC…) for the target years and category — verify the title, studio, year, and award level against that source. Third-party roundups are leads to verify, not citations.
3. **Separate the philosophy from the pixels.** Where the designer/studio has stated their thinking (case study, interview), quote it briefly and attribute it. Where they haven't, say the philosophy is your reading, not their claim.
4. **Dissect the design logic.** Concept (the idea that carries it), system (grid, hierarchy, type pairing, colour rationale), and craft (production, material, finish). Name *why* each choice serves the concept — a winner is usually a tight loop between idea and execution.
5. **Read what the jury rewarded.** Award levels and categories encode a value (craft vs concept vs cultural impact). Note which axis each piece won on — it tells you what "good" meant in that room.
6. **Build a comparative reference, not a single hero.** 5–8 pieces that triangulate the space, with a one-line "what to steal" (the transferable principle) per piece — the principle, never the artwork.
7. **Respect currency.** Prioritise the last ~5 years so the reference reflects current craft; flag anything older as a classic, not a trend.
8. **Credit relentlessly.** Studio, designer, client, year, award — every reference carries its attribution, so your moodboard is a citation, not a theft.

## Quality bar

Winners are verified against the official award archive (title, studio, year, level) · stated philosophy is quoted and attributed, inferred philosophy is labelled · design logic is dissected at concept / system / craft · the axis the jury rewarded is named · the reference is comparative with a transferable principle each · recency is prioritised · every piece is credited.

## Guardrails & escalation

Never reproduce the awarded artwork or present its distinctive style as your own original work — reference and cite, then make something new. Award facts (who won what, when) are verified against the primary source; if the official record is ambiguous, say so rather than guessing. When a reference would inform a client-facing claim ("award-winning approach"), keep the line clear between *inspired by* and *affiliated with* — the latter is a legal statement, not a design one.
